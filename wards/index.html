Saint Alphonsa of the Immaculate Conception is the first woman of Indian origin to be canonized by the Catholic Church and the first saint of the Syro-Malabar Church. Born as Anna Muttathupadathu in 1910 in Kerala, she joined the Franciscan Clarist Congregation and dedicated her life to teaching and prayer. Despite lifelong illness and suffering, she was known for her deep faith, humility, and joyful acceptance of hardship. She was beatified by Pope John Paul II in 1986 and canonized by Pope Benedict XVI on 12 October 2008. Her tomb at Bharananganam has become an important pilgrimage site for the faithful.

Saint Euphrasia Eluvathingal (born Rosa Eluvathingal, 17 October 1877 – 29 August 1952) was an Indian Carmelite nun and mystic known as “the Praying Mother.” She entered the Congregation of the Mother of Carmel, lived most of her life in the convent at Ollur, and spent over 50 years confined to the cloister despite suffering many illnesses. She is remembered for her deep humility, hidden life of prayer and penance, her care for others especially the sick and destitute, and her patience in the face of hardship. Her tomb at Ollur has become a pilgrim centre for thousands who come seeking her intercessory prayers.

Saint Anthony of Padua (born Fernando Martins de Bulhões, 15 August 1195 – 13 June 1231) was a Portuguese Catholic priest and Franciscan friar renowned for his preaching, knowledge of Scripture, and care for the poor. He was born in Lisbon into a noble family and first joined the Augustinian Canons before entering the Franciscan Order, inspired by the martyrdom of friars in Morocco. Known as a gifted preacher and teacher, he became one of the most popular saints of the Church, often invoked as the patron of lost items. His holiness was confirmed by many miracles attributed to his intercession during his life and after his death. Canonized in 1232, just a year after his passing, he was later declared a Doctor of the Church in 1946. His feast day is celebrated on 13 June, the day of his death.

Saint Michael the Archangel is one of the principal angels in Christian tradition, revered as the leader of the heavenly hosts and protector of the faithful. His name means “Who is like God?” and he is celebrated as a defender against evil and the great adversary of Satan. In Scripture, he appears in the Book of Daniel as the protector of God’s people, in the Epistle of Jude as the archangel who contends with the devil, and in the Book of Revelation as the warrior who defeats the dragon. He is often depicted in armor, wielding a sword or spear, and standing victorious over Satan. Honored as the patron of soldiers, police, and all who fight evil, he is invoked for protection and strength. His feast day, shared with the archangels Gabriel and Raphael, is celebrated on 29 September.

Saint Francis de Sales was a French Catholic bishop, spiritual writer, and Doctor of the Church, revered for his gentleness, wisdom, and devotion. Born in Savoy in 1567, he first studied law before pursuing the priesthood, and later became Bishop of Geneva in 1602. He is celebrated for his patient and compassionate approach during the Counter-Reformation, bringing many back to the Catholic faith through preaching and personal care. He authored spiritual classics such as Introduction to the Devout Life and Treatise on the Love of God, which continue to guide Christians in living a life of holiness. He co-founded the Order of the Visitation of Holy Mary with Saint Jane Frances de Chantal, offering a path of devotion marked by humility and simplicity. Honored as the patron of writers, journalists, and the deaf, he was canonized in 1665 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1877. His feast day is celebrated on 24 January.

Saint George (died 23 April 303) was a Roman soldier and Christian martyr, venerated as one of the most prominent military saints. Born in Cappadocia (modern-day Turkey), he served in the Roman army under Emperor Diocletian. When the emperor issued an edict persecuting Christians, George publicly declared his faith, distributing his wealth to the poor and denouncing the persecution. For his steadfastness, he endured torture and was ultimately beheaded in Lydda (now Lod, Israel). His legend, particularly the tale of slaying a dragon to rescue a princess, symbolizes the triumph of good over evil. Saint George is honored as the patron saint of England, Georgia, and several other regions and professions. His feast day is celebrated on 23 April.

Saint Joseph (c. 98 BC – c. AD 1) was a Jewish carpenter and the earthly father of Jesus Christ, venerated as a model of humility, obedience, and fatherly care. He was betrothed to Mary, the mother of Jesus, and faithfully protected and provided for the Holy Family, following divine guidance through dreams. Joseph is celebrated for his righteous and silent witness to God’s will, working diligently as a carpenter and nurturing Jesus in his early life. He is honored as the patron saint of workers, fathers, and the universal Church. His feast days are celebrated on 19 March (Saint Joseph, Husband of Mary) and 1 May (Saint Joseph the Worker).

Saint Jude the Apostle (also known as Thaddeus, Judas Thaddaeus) was one of the Twelve Apostles of Jesus Christ, venerated as a model of faith, courage, and missionary zeal. He is traditionally credited with writing the Epistle of Jude in the New Testament, which warns against false teachings and encourages perseverance in faith. Jude is celebrated for his evangelization efforts, traveling widely to spread the Gospel, and for his unwavering commitment to Christ even in the face of persecution. He is honored as the patron saint of lost causes, desperate situations, and hopeless cases. His feast day is celebrated on 28 October in the Western Church and on 19 June and 30 June in the Eastern Church.

Saint Kuriakose Elias Chavara (10 February 1805 – 3 January 1871) was a pioneering Indian Catholic priest, social reformer, and the first canonized Catholic male saint of Indian origin. Born in Kainakary, Kerala, he was ordained a priest in 1829 and became a visionary leader in the Syro-Malabar Church. In 1831, he co-founded the Carmelites of Mary Immaculate (CMI), the first indigenous male religious congregation in India, and later supported the establishment of the Congregation of the Mother of Carmel (CMC) for women. Chavara was instrumental in promoting education, founding the first Catholic Sanskrit school and encouraging every church to start a school. He also established a printing press to disseminate Catholic literature and initiated social welfare programs for the destitute. Canonized by Pope Francis on 23 November 2014, his feast day is celebrated on 3 January in the Syro-Malabar Church and on 18 February in the Roman Catholic Church.

Mary, Mother of Jesus (c. 18–16 BC – after AD 30) was a Jewish woman of Nazareth, betrothed to Joseph, and the mother of Jesus Christ. She is venerated as the Mother of God in Christian tradition and holds a central role in the faith. Mary is honored for her obedience to God’s will, her role in the Incarnation, and her presence at significant events in Jesus’ life, including his crucifixion. She is also revered in Islam, where she is considered the greatest of all women. Mary’s titles include Theotokos (God-bearer), Queen of Heaven, and Mediatrix. Her feast days are celebrated on 15 August (Assumption) and 8 September (Nativity).

Saint Teresa of Calcutta (born Agnes Gonxha Bojaxhiu, 26 August 1910 – 5 September 1997) was an Albanian-Indian Catholic nun and missionary renowned for her selfless service to the poor, sick, and destitute in Kolkata (Calcutta), India. She founded the Missionaries of Charity in 1950, a congregation dedicated to helping the “poorest of the poor.” Her unwavering commitment to humanitarian work earned her the Nobel Peace Prize in 1979. Despite facing criticism for her approach to suffering and medical care, her legacy continues through the global network of Missionaries of Charity centers. She was canonized by Pope Francis on 4 September 2016, and her feast day is celebrated on 5 September, the anniversary of her death.

Saint Peter the Apostle (c. 1 – c. 67 AD) was a Jewish fisherman from Bethsaida, the son of Jonah, and the brother of Andrew. He is recognized as the leader of Jesus Christ’s Twelve Apostles and is considered the first Pope of the Roman Catholic Church. Peter is celebrated for his bold faith, his role in spreading Christianity, and his leadership in the early Church. He is often depicted holding keys, symbolizing his role in the Church, and is the patron saint of fishermen, netmakers, and shipbuilders. Saint Peter is believed to have been crucified upside down in Rome during the reign of Emperor Nero, around 64–67 AD. His feast day is celebrated on 29 June.

Saint Sebastian (c. 256 – c. 288 AD) was a Christian martyr and saint who served as a captain in the Roman army during the reign of Emperor Diocletian. Despite the emperor’s persecution of Christians, Sebastian secretly converted many to Christianity. When his faith was discovered, he was sentenced to death and was tied to a tree and shot with arrows. Miraculously surviving this ordeal, he confronted the emperor, leading to his subsequent martyrdom by being clubbed to death. Saint Sebastian is often depicted in art as a young man bound to a tree and pierced with arrows. He is venerated as the patron saint of athletes, archers, and those suffering from plague. His feast day is celebrated on 20 January in the Catholic Church.

Saint Thomas the Apostle (c. 1 – c. 72 AD), also known as Didymus (“the Twin”), was one of the Twelve Apostles of Jesus Christ. He is best known for his initial doubt regarding Jesus’ resurrection, famously declaring, “Unless I see in His hands the print of the nails, and place my finger into the mark of the nails, and place my hand into His side, I will not believe” (John 20:25). Upon witnessing the risen Christ, he professed, “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28). Tradition holds that Thomas traveled to India in 52 AD to spread the Gospel, establishing Christian communities along the Malabar Coast, particularly in Kerala. He is revered as the patron saint of architects and builders. Saint Thomas was martyred in Mylapore (modern-day Chennai, India) around 72 AD. His feast day is celebrated on 3 July in the Syro-Malabar Catholic Church and other Christian denominations.

Saint Vincent de Paul (24 April 1581 – 27 September 1660) was a French Catholic priest renowned for his dedication to serving the poor and marginalized. Born into a peasant family in Pouy, Gascony, he was ordained a priest in 1600 and later studied theology at the University of Toulouse. After being captured by pirates and enslaved for two years, he returned to France and became a parish priest. Deeply moved by the plight of the poor, he founded the Congregation of the Mission (Vincentians) in 1625 to evangelize and train clergy, and co-founded the Daughters of Charity with Saint Louise de Marillac in 1633 to serve the needy. Vincent was instrumental in organizing charitable works, founding hospitals, and ransoming galley slaves. He was canonized in 1737, and his feast day is celebrated on 27 September.
